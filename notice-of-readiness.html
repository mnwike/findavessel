Notice of readiness

Notice of readiness, or NOR, is the notice a ship’s master gives the charterer that the ship has arrived and is ready to load or discharge. It is what starts the laytime clock.

Three conditions for a valid notice

  1. The ship has arrived at the place the charter party names. Under a berth charter that is the berth; under a port charter, the usual waiting area within the port.
  2. The ship is ready in every way that matters for the cargo: holds or tanks clean and passed, equipment working, health and customs clearance where required.
  3. The notice is given as the contract requires: to the right party, by the agreed method, and within any office hours the charter party sets.

A notice given before the ship is actually ready is generally invalid, and it does not become valid later. A fresh notice is needed.

Why it causes so many disputes

Days of laytime and tens of thousands of dollars of demurrage can turn on whether a notice was valid. Typical arguments: holds failed inspection after the notice was given, the ship was waiting outside port limits, or the notice was sent outside permitted hours.

Terms that modify the rule

Charter parties often let notice be given "whether in berth or not" or "whether in port or not", so the owner is not penalised when the port is congested. Tanker charters usually give six hours after notice before laytime runs.

Good practice

  • Owners: tender on arrival, and tender again if anything about readiness changes.
  • Charterers: if you think a notice is invalid, say so in writing at once.
  • Both: make sure the time of the notice is in the statement of facts.
