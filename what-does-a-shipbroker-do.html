What does a shipbroker do?

A shipbroker is an intermediary who finds a ship for a cargo, or a cargo for a ship, negotiates the terms between the two sides and follows the deal through. They are paid commission on the freight.

What a broker actually does

  • Knows the market. Which ships are open where, which cargoes are quoting, and what rates were done yesterday.
  • Circulates the enquiry to owners or charterers who might fit, without exposing more than the principal wants.
  • Negotiates offers and counter-offers, and advises on what is reasonable.
  • Draws up the recap and charter party.
  • Follows the voyage: passes on notices, chases freight, handles the laytime calculation and helps settle disagreements.

How commission works

The usual brokerage is 1.25% of gross freight or hire for each broker in the chain. The owner pays it, deducted from freight. Some charterers also take an address commission for themselves, often 1.25% to 3.75%. Total commissions of 2.5% to 5% are common, and the owner prices them into the freight rate.

Types of shipbroker

Chartering brokers arrange charters and usually specialise by ship type: dry cargo, tanker or gas. Sale and purchase brokers handle buying and selling ships. A competitive broker works one deal for both sides; exclusive brokers act for one principal only.

The problem newcomers run into

Brokers are paid on completed deals, so they give their time to enquiries likely to fix. An unknown name with a first cargo often gets no reply. The fix is to remove the doubt: show who you are, that the cargo or ship is real and that you can pay or perform. That is what a FindAVessel passport is for.

What to ask a broker before you instruct them

  • Who do you act for in this deal: me, the other side, or both?
  • How many intermediaries are between you and the principal?
  • What is the total commission, and who receives each part?
  • Do you carry professional indemnity insurance?
