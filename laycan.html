Laycan: laydays and cancelling

Laycan is short for laydays and cancelling. It is the window of dates in which the ship must arrive at the load port, ready to load. Arrive after it and the charterer may cancel.

The two dates

The first layday is the earliest date the charterer has to accept the ship. If she arrives sooner she waits, and laytime does not start until that date unless the charterer agrees.

The cancelling date is the latest. If the ship has not arrived and given a valid notice of readiness by then, the charterer has the option to cancel the charter. It is an option, not an automatic end: the charterer can also keep the ship, often after renegotiating.

Why the window matters to each side

  • The charterer has a cargo that is ready on certain dates, storage that costs money and a sale contract with a shipment period. A late ship can put them in breach with their buyer.
  • The owner has a ship finishing a previous voyage somewhere else. The window has to be reachable allowing for weather, port delays and bunkering.

How wide should a laycan be?

Five to ten days is common for dry bulk, narrower for tankers. A tight window gives the charterer certainty and costs the owner flexibility, which shows up in the freight rate. A cargo posted with a realistic window attracts more offers.

If the ship is going to be late

Many charter parties have an interpellation clause. The owner tells the charterer the new expected date and the charterer must say, within a set time, whether they will cancel. This stops the ship steaming for days towards a cargo that has already gone elsewhere.

Laycan in an offer

An owner can offer a narrower window inside the one the charterer posted, for example 12 to 14 December against 10 to 15. Check that the offered dates still fit your cargo before accepting.
