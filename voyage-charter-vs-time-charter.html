Voyage, time and bareboat charters

There are three ways to charter a whole ship. They differ in who runs the ship and who pays for what. Most first deals are voyage charters.

Voyage charter

The charterer pays freight to move a cargo from A to B, quoted per tonne or as a lump sum. The owner pays for fuel, ports and crew and carries the risk of the voyage taking longer at sea. The charterer's main exposure is time in port: exceed the laytime and demurrage is due.

Use it when you have a specific cargo and want a known cost per tonne.

Time charter

The charterer hires the ship by the day for a period, from one trip to several years, and directs where it goes. The owner still supplies crew and maintains the ship. The charterer pays hire plus fuel, port costs and canal tolls. If the ship breaks down it goes "off-hire" and the charterer stops paying.

Use it when you have a programme of cargoes, or want control over the ship's schedule.

Bareboat charter

Also called a demise charter. The charterer takes the ship without crew and operates it as if they owned it, usually for years. It is closer to a lease than a transport contract, and is often used to finance ships. Most web searches for "bareboat charter" are about yachts, where the word simply means hiring a boat without a skipper.

The people involved

TermMeaning
ChartererThe party hiring the ship: a cargo owner, a trader or an operator.
ShipownerThe registered owner of the vessel.
Disponent ownerA company that controls the ship under a time or bareboat charter and charters it out again. To the next charterer they stand in the owner’s place.
OperatorA company that takes ships on time charter and carries other people’s cargoes on voyage terms.

Comparing a voyage with a time charter

Owners compare the two by converting a voyage into a time charter equivalent: voyage revenue minus fuel, port costs and commissions, divided by the days the voyage takes. The voyage estimator does this sum.
